TNFD and TCFD: what nature and climate data your company needs to collect

The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) and the Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD) represent the convergence of two trends: the mainstreaming of ESG as a financial risk framework, and the regulatory push to make non-financial disclosure mandatory, verifiable, and comparable.

Both frameworks require data. Specific, documented, verifiable data — not narrative commitments or self-certified scores.

TCFD: the established framework

TCFD was established in 2015 by the Financial Stability Board and published its recommendations in 2017. Since then, adoption has moved from voluntary to mandatory in a growing number of major economies.

The TCFD framework organises climate-related disclosure around four pillars: governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets. The metrics and targets pillar is where data governance becomes critical.

What TCFD requires companies to disclose:

  • Scope 1, 2, and 3 greenhouse gas emissions (direct emissions, purchased energy, and supply chain)
  • Climate-related transition risks (regulatory, technology, market, reputational)
  • Physical risks from climate change (acute events and chronic shifts)
  • Progress against any climate-related targets, including net zero commitments
  • How the board oversees climate-related risks and opportunities

The data challenge: Scope 3 emissions — supply chain emissions — are the largest category for most companies and the hardest to measure. They require data from suppliers, logistics providers, product lifecycle assessments, and end-of-life disposal. Companies that have not yet mapped their supply chain emissions data sources face a significant data collection and governance challenge.

Physical risk data requires access to location-specific climate projections, flood maps, heat stress indices, and extreme weather probability data — most of which exists in some form through government agencies, insurance data, and satellite-derived products, but needs to be assembled into a governed form to support disclosure.

TNFD: the nature framework

TNFD published its final recommendations in September 2023, following the TCFD structure with four pillars — governance, strategy, risk and impact management, and metrics and targets — adapted for nature-related risks and dependencies.

The TNFD adds a fifth element that TCFD does not have: the LEAP approach (Locate, Evaluate, Assess, Prepare), a methodology for assessing how a company interacts with nature at specific locations.

What TNFD requires companies to disclose:

  • Nature-related dependencies: what natural systems does the business depend on? (Pollinators for agricultural inputs, freshwater for manufacturing, coastal ecosystems for storm protection)
  • Nature-related impacts: what effects does the business have on natural systems? (Land conversion, water use, pollution, invasive species introduction)
  • Nature-related risks: how do dependencies and impacts translate into financial risk?
  • Metrics aligned to the global biodiversity framework: area of land managed for nature, species affected, ecosystem condition

The data challenge for TNFD: Most organisations do not have location-specific data about their natural environment dependencies and impacts. The LEAP approach requires site-level assessment — not just corporate-level aggregates — which means companies need spatial data about the ecological context of their operating locations.

This is where EO data, biosphere monitoring data, and local biodiversity records become directly relevant to corporate disclosure. The data that a Biosphere Reserve like the Isle of Man collects through its Nature Recovery Network and BAP programme is exactly the kind of location-specific, ecosystem-condition data that TNFD disclosures require.

The common data governance requirement

Both TCFD and TNFD share a fundamental data governance requirement: the data behind disclosures must be verifiable.

Self-certified numbers in an annual report are increasingly insufficient. Investors, auditors, and regulators are asking for the underlying data — its provenance, its methodology, and the evidence that it accurately represents the organisation’s real-world conditions.

An organisation that can say “our Scope 3 emissions figure comes from this data source, collected by this methodology, validated by this third party, and registered as a governed data asset” is in a fundamentally different compliance position from one that produces a number derived from estimates and industry averages.

Minimum governance requirements for TCFD/TNFD data:

  1. Source documentation — where each data point comes from, and who collected it
  2. Methodology record — how the data was processed and what assumptions were applied
  3. Quality assessment — the accuracy and completeness of the underlying data
  4. Third-party validation — for material metrics, independent verification of methodology and outputs
  5. Temporal record — when the data was collected and how current it is
  6. Change documentation — if methodology or scope changes between reporting periods, a clear explanation of the change

These are the characteristics of a governed data asset. They are not a new burden imposed by TCFD and TNFD — they are the governance infrastructure that converts compliance reporting from a risk into a competitive advantage.

Companies that have governed their environmental data assets before disclosure requirements become mandatory will complete their first regulatory submissions faster, at lower cost, and with less audit risk than those scrambling to assemble data at the last minute.
