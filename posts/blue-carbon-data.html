Blue carbon: what the data is, where it comes from, and what it is worth

Seagrass meadows, saltmarshes, mangroves, and kelp forests sequester carbon at rates that can exceed terrestrial forests per unit area. They are also among the least well-governed data assets in the environmental economy.

Blue carbon data — the measurements, models, and evidence that document carbon storage in marine and coastal ecosystems — is in high demand from investors, regulators, and conservation organisations. Supply of verified, governed blue carbon data is far shorter than demand. That gap is an opportunity.

What blue carbon data consists of

Blue carbon accounting requires several categories of data, each with different collection methodologies and quality standards:

Carbon stock data. Measurements of the carbon stored in above-ground biomass (plant material) and below-ground organic matter (soil and sediment). Sediment carbon stocks are typically much larger than biomass stocks in blue carbon habitats — saltmarsh and seagrass sediments can store carbon accumulated over centuries. Stock measurement requires physical sampling: sediment cores, biomass sampling, laboratory analysis.

Flux data. Measurements of carbon exchange between the ecosystem and the atmosphere — how much carbon the habitat is absorbing versus releasing. This requires continuous or frequent monitoring, typically using eddy covariance towers for saltmarsh or flux chambers for smaller-scale measurements.

Extent and condition data. The area of habitat present and its ecological condition. A degraded seagrass meadow has different carbon storage characteristics from a healthy one. Extent data comes from aerial survey or satellite remote sensing; condition assessment requires either in-situ survey or high-resolution imagery analysis.

Historical change data. Records of how the habitat extent and condition have changed over time — critical for establishing additionality in carbon credit schemes. A habitat that has been stable for decades has different credit potential from one being actively restored from a degraded state.

The Isle of Man blue carbon evidence base

The Isle of Man’s Manx Blue Carbon Project ran from 2022 to 2026. The project documented the island’s coastal blue carbon habitats, with Isle of Man Government reporting estimating storage of approximately 500,000 tonnes of carbon — equivalent to around 1.8 million tonnes of CO2 — in these habitats.

The project generated economic and social gains alongside the environmental evidence, including supporting a sustainable Nephrops (langoustine) fishery, tourism activity, and educational outputs. This multi-benefit profile is characteristic of well-documented blue carbon ecosystems: the data underpins not just carbon accounting but a broader natural capital valuation.

This evidence base is an asset. But its commercial potential depends on whether it is governed — provenanced, quality-assured, and registered — in a way that allows it to underpin nature finance transactions, attract research investment, or support regulatory compliance claims.

What makes blue carbon data commercially valuable

Verification standard compliance. Several voluntary carbon market standards include blue carbon methodologies: the Verified Carbon Standard (Verra VCS), the Gold Standard, and developing frameworks for specific habitat types. Data collected to the methodology requirements of a recognised standard is significantly more valuable than equivalent data collected without reference to a standard — because it is the standard compliance that allows credits to be issued.

Longitudinal depth. Blue carbon habitats accumulate carbon over long periods. Data that documents stocks built up over decades or centuries requires sediment coring that cannot be replicated quickly. Historical data is inherently scarce and its value increases over time.

Spatial precision. Advances in satellite imagery resolution — now approaching 25 centimetres for commercial providers — combined with spectral analysis and AI-applied classification can produce habitat maps at resolutions that were impossible from space ten years ago. Spatial data derived from high-resolution satellite imagery is both more precise and more updateable than data derived from periodic aerial survey.

Chain of custody. A nature finance buyer needs to be able to trace the credit they are purchasing back to the specific measurement, methodology, and location that it represents. A clear, documented chain of custody — from field measurement through data processing to credit issuance — is what makes a blue carbon credit tradeable rather than contested.

The governance gap

The most common reason blue carbon data does not convert into blue carbon value is not a lack of data. It is a lack of governance.

Organisations that have invested in blue carbon surveys often hold their data in project-specific databases, report it once, and then move on. The data is not registered as an asset. Its provenance is not formally documented. Its methodology is recorded in a project report that exists in a filing system nobody can navigate. When a nature finance buyer asks for the data package, there is no data package — there is a collection of files, spreadsheets, and reports that require months to assemble and validate.

Data asset governance applied to blue carbon data changes this. A governed blue carbon data asset has a persistent identifier, a documented methodology, a verified chain of custody, and a registered owner who can provide a due-diligence-ready data room in days rather than months.

That is the difference between a blue carbon survey and a blue carbon asset.
